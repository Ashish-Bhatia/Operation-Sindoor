# International legal issues arising from the 2025 India–Pakistan confrontation

**Investigation checkpoint 03 · 19 October 2026**  
**Status:** Preliminary legal memorandum based on public records and authoritative texts. It is not a judicial finding and does not determine the legality of any specific use of force.

## A. Identify the distinct legal questions

1. **Jus ad bellum:** Did either state have a legal basis under the UN Charter for resorting to interstate force on a particular date?
2. **Jus in bello / international humanitarian law (IHL):** During an armed conflict, did each specific operation comply with the rules protecting civilians and civilian objects?
3. **Attribution and state responsibility:** What publicly testable facts connect the Pahalgam attackers or other armed groups to individuals, organizations or Pakistani state organs?
4. **Individual criminal responsibility:** What conduct by named individuals has been lawfully charged, what evidence supports it, and has a competent court reached a finding?
5. **Treaty consequences:** What legal grounds, if any, support suspension or abeyance of the Indus Waters Treaty? This is separate from justifying kinetic military action.

These are not interchangeable tests. A party potentially entitled to self-defence can still commit an unlawful individual attack. Conversely, compliance with IHL does not itself establish a lawful entitlement to begin a war.

## B. Authoritative primary rules

The [United Nations Charter Article 2(4)](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/un-charter/chapter-1) generally prohibits force against another state's territorial integrity or political independence. [Article 51](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/un-charter/chapter-7) preserves the right of individual or collective self-defence if an armed attack occurs, pending relevant Security Council measures, and requires states to report self-defence measures to the Council.

A self-defence justification requires assessment of factual predicate, necessity and proportionality under jus ad bellum. Whether and on what basis Article 51 encompasses attacks by non-state armed groups operating from another state's territory is a contested area of doctrine. The so-called 'unable or unwilling' justification for using force without territorial-state consent is accepted by some states and commentators but not universally accepted as a settled rule.

An [ICJ judgment involving Uganda and the Democratic Republic of Congo](https://www.icj-cij.org/case/116) rejected Uganda's claimed self-defence on the facts before that court. It is relevant to legal analysis, but it does not mechanically resolve the India–Pakistan events of 2025, which have their own factual disputes.

The [ICRC customary IHL database](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/customary-ihl/v1) sets out the rules on **distinction** (including military-objective identification), **proportionality in attack** and **feasible precautions**. The [ICRC explanatory FAQ](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/FAQ-rules-of-war-ihl?language=en) clarifies their application to civilian harm. Applicable IHL is independent of which side began hostilities.

## C. India’s publicly stated legal and factual case

India attributed the 22 April Pahalgam attack to a Pakistan-linked armed-group network. India's 7 May briefing publicly characterized its initial strikes as a limited response targeting facilities associated with those groups and described further threats. Its diplomats later spoke explicitly of self-defence. The publicly released material must be evaluated for:

- Proof of the actual perpetrators and the claimed cross-border support.
- Whether the attack met the relevant armed-attack threshold.
- What direct evidence of an imminent or continuing threat existed when decisions were taken.
- Whether other effective options were available, relevant to necessity.
- Whether the level, object and continuation of force were proportionate to lawful defensive ends.
- The legal treatment of force against non-state groups inside another state's territory without that state's consent.

The later NIA chargesheets are important prosecution records but did not exist as public filed documents on 7 May. Evidence developed afterward cannot simply be treated as if all of it was already available to decision-makers at that time.

## D. Pakistan’s stated legal and factual case

In its [10 May official statement](https://pid.gov.pk/site/press_detail/29017), Pakistan expressly invoked **Article 51**, characterizing its military response as self-defence against allegedly unlawful Indian strikes and harm to civilians. Pakistan disputed the prior attribution of the Pahalgam attack, and proposed what it described as an independent investigation.

This establishes the existence and text of Pakistan's legal case. It does not settle whether all military responses were necessary, proportionate, properly targeted or compliant with civilian protections. Each separate episode of force must be examined on its own facts.

## E. Legal examination of particular reported strike sites

Under IHL, historical institutional affiliation or geographical proximity does not alone show that a specific building was a military objective *at the time of the attack*. An object normally regarded as civilian might become a military objective if it makes an effective contribution to military action and attacking it offers a definite military advantage in the circumstances. The attack would still have to satisfy precautions and proportionality.

Conversely, labeling a building religious, educational or residential does not by itself determine whether its specific use at the relevant time made it a military objective. Public photos of rubble do not demonstrate the legal status of the building, the information available to decision-makers, or foreseeable civilian harm.

Each incident must therefore record:
- Dated, authenticated evidence of use of the specific object.
- The legal status of people allegedly present, with presumption of civilian protection where doubt exists.
- The information reasonably available to the decision-maker when the attack was ordered.
- Verified civilian injuries, fatalities and property damage; no presumption of zero from missing data.
- Independent evidence of precautions and changes in circumstances.
- Competing explanations and the limits of what the public record permits.

This investigation will not publicize sensitive current defensive information.

## F. Distinguish legal terminology

**Necessity and proportionality in jus ad bellum** concern whether and to what extent self-defensive force was needed to meet a lawful defensive end. **Proportionality in IHL** instead concerns anticipated incidental civilian harm relative to concrete and direct military advantage from a particular attack. Neither is equivalent to equal numbers of casualties on both sides or matching the adversary's strike count.

An assertion that strikes were 'precise' is not a legal substitute for distinction and precautions. A claim that attacks were 'retaliatory' is not sufficient by itself to establish self-defence. A claim that a site was 'terrorist infrastructure' is a proposed factual attribution, not proof of every building's status.

## G. Interpreting official and independent reporting

An authenticated government briefing proves the government issued that briefing. It does not establish the truth of its military success or civilian-harm figures. Independent satellite damage documentation confirms physical changes but often does not reveal whether military personnel or civilians were present, whether a facility was functioning, or what warnings and precautions were taken.

The report therefore cannot make a defensible final judgment on the initial 7 May strikes or all subsequent episodes without the underlying threat intelligence, target-purpose evidence and incident-specific civilian records.

## I. Highest-value next records

1. Any contemporaneous **Article 51 notifications** to the Security Council by India or Pakistan and the UN document identifiers.
2. Dated material supporting claims of future attack threats and responsibility, where lawfully public.
3. The filed NIA prosecution exhibits, forensic provenance and later judicial orders.
4. Authenticated contemporaneous records of the actual use of each initial strike object.
5. Verified civilian/medical records, property impact and response timelines on both sides.
6. Original full treaty/arbitration decisions relevant to water law, examined separately.

**Editorial conclusion:** No party's invocation of the Charter automatically establishes the lawful resort to force. No party's claimed military objective automatically establishes the legality of the specific strike. The commission's final legal determination remains open.
